Why North Gaza

We deliberately concentrate on the north – where the need is greatest and the least help arrives.

The Yellow Line

With the ceasefire of October 2025, the Israeli army withdrew behind a marked line, the so-called “Yellow Line”. Behind it lies, according to the UN, around 53% of the Gaza Strip; if you add the areas under Israeli control or with access restrictions, it is 64.9%.1

In October 2026, more than 100 aid organisations found that the line had been moved inward and that the area controlled by Israel had grown from about 53% to about 63%.2 In October 2026, the UN again reported that markers had been moved westward – to where displaced people live.3 Our animal welfare project is located in North Gaza, in the Yellow Zone.

What is demonstrably happening there

The UN Human Rights Office (OHCHR) verified 1,076 killings in Gaza from the start of the ceasefire to the end of July 2026, including 268 children. 331 of them happened near the Yellow Line. According to the Office, many of those killed posed no discernible threat – in June 2026, for example, a two-year-old boy was shot in his father's arms 150–200 metres west of the line.4

How international bodies assess this

International courts and UN bodies have commented as follows on the conduct in the Palestinian territories and in Gaza:

  • The International Court of Justice found in 2024 that Israel's continued presence in the occupied Palestinian territories is unlawful.5
  • In November 2024, the International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants against Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant – on suspicion of war crimes and crimes against humanity, including starvation as a method of warfare. Arrest warrants are not a conviction; they remain in force.6
  • The UN Commission of Inquiry found in September 2025 that genocide is being committed in Gaza, and in October 2026 that this continues even after the ceasefire – a finding of a UN body, not a court judgment. Israel rejects this as disinformation.7
That is why North Gaza: According to the findings of the United Nations, civilians continue to be killed precisely along the Yellow Line, and international courts and UN bodies see the gravest violations of international law – and at the same time, the least help arrives right there. We want to be where others don't reach: for the people and for the animals.

What this means for daily life

Near the line, every journey is dangerous – to fetch water, to go shopping, to see the vet. Help is harder to deliver: the World Food Programme was most recently able to distribute hot meals near the Yellow Line only every other day.8 Then there is winter: in the first winter storm in early November 2026, up to 46,000 households were affected, according to the UN, and six people died.1

For our work this means: Food, medicines and building materials are hard to get and expensive. Rescues often take place among rubble and at risk to life. And the team that looks after the animals lives under these conditions itself.
